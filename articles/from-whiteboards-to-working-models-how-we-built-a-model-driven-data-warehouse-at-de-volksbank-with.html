From Whiteboards to Working Models: How We Built a Model-Driven Data Warehouse at de Volksbank with CrossBreeze

2025-09-19 · 5 min read

Why teamwork and model-driven engineering delivered a future-proof data warehouse under regulatory pressure

Summary

At de Volksbank (parent of ASN Bank and RegioBank), we replaced the Risk & Finance Data Warehouse under strict ECB/DNB requirements. Working with CrossBreeze, we applied Model Driven Data Engineering (MDDE) with PowerDesigner data models as the single source of truth.

Alongside PowerDesigner, we relied on CrossBreeze's CrossTest for automated validation and the open-source PowerDecomposer to unlock metadata-driven automation.

The lessons we learned there also shaped CrossModel and CrossGenerate, CrossBreeze's next-generation tools.

This case study is about what we built, why the team made it work, and why Structure Beats Magic isn't a slogan when a regulator is checking your lineage.

The Challenge

Replacing a Risk & Finance DWH is not business as usual. The demands were steep:

  • Meet regulatory deadlines from ECB/DNB.
  • Provide lineage and governance an auditor could trust without a walkthrough.
  • Ensure quality and consistency across multiple domains feeding the same reports.
  • Deliver a platform that was faster and more maintainable than the ETL-heavy system it replaced.

Hand-coded ETL, scattered documentation, and manual testing don't scale under that combination. Every one of those four demands is really the same demand: prove the system does what you say it does, on command, without a rebuild. That's a structure problem, not a talent problem — no amount of individual brilliance in hand-written SQL produces lineage an auditor can trust. You need the model to carry the proof.

The Model-Driven Approach

Instead of coding everything by hand, we adopted Model Driven Data Engineering (MDDE):

  • PowerDesigner models became the single source of truth for structure, mappings, and business rules.
  • A metadata repository captured everything — no hidden logic living only in someone's head or a Confluence page.
  • Generators and automation frameworks turned models into SQL code and automated tests.
  • CI/CD pipelines made deployments reproducible and safe.

The idea was simple: if it's in the model, it's in the code. That single rule is what makes MDDE a tool-extender rather than a rewrite of the stack — PowerDesigner, SQL, and CI/CD were all tools the team already knew. What changed was the discipline of routing every change through the model first, so the model stayed the one place a reviewer needed to look.

The Role of CrossBreeze

We heavily used CrossTest, CrossBreeze's testing framework. It let us embed automated regression and functional tests directly into the data warehouse pipelines, so every release shipped with evidence, not just confidence.

We also relied on PowerDecomposer, an open-source tool CrossBreeze built: 👉 PowerDecomposer. It decomposed PowerDesigner models into usable metadata structures, connecting modeling to automation and governance. Without it, PowerDesigner's models would have stayed diagrams — pretty, but inert. PowerDecomposer is what turned them into metadata-as-code: structure a generator could act on, not just a picture a human could read.

At the same time, CrossBreeze was already building CrossModel and CrossGenerate — tools born directly from the gaps we hit on this project.

  • PowerDesigner, while solid, had limits: SAP had stopped prioritizing it, development had slowed, and metadata management and automation had visible gaps.
  • Based on this project (and others), CrossBreeze started building CrossModel as a modern, metadata-native replacement for PowerDesigner, ER/Studio, and similar legacy modeling tools.
  • Alongside it, CrossGenerate took shape: generating code directly from metadata models instead of through a decomposition step bolted on afterward.

The Volksbank project wasn't just a delivery — it was a feedback loop into better tools. That's what a healthy tool-extender relationship with a vendor looks like: you don't wait for the framework to be perfect, you use what exists, and the friction you hit becomes the vendor's next roadmap item.

Results That Spoke for Themselves

The combination of strong people, solid tools, and disciplined modeling delivered results that didn't need spin:

  • 3x faster development speed compared to traditional coding.
  • Onboarding time dropped from 3 months to 3 weeks — because new team members could read the model, not reverse-engineer someone else's SQL.
  • 10,000+ automated tests and controls embedded into the platform, not bolted on at the end.
  • Production go-live in 2.5 years, meeting every regulatory deadline.

The number that mattered most internally wasn't any of those — it was confidence: in the data, in the lineage, and in the reproducibility of every release. That's the actual product of structure. Speed and onboarding time are downstream effects.

Why the Team Was the Key

The technology was necessary, but it wasn't sufficient. The real success came from the team dynamic:

  • Volksbank colleagues who believed in modeling-first, even when it meant slower to a first-and-wrong answer.
  • CrossBreeze experts who brought deep product knowledge and fixed gaps in real time.
  • A shared vision, stated plainly: move from whiteboards to working models.

Complex data platforms don't get solved by one brilliant engineer with more coffee. It took a team of committed people rowing in the same direction, with a model as the shared reference point — so disagreements were about the model, not about whose SQL was right.

Lessons Learned

Three things held up on reflection, months later:

1. Model-driven engineering works — when models drive structure, mappings, and rules, both quality and speed improve, and they improve together instead of trading off.

2. Automation pays off — code generation and testing aren't luxuries you add once budget allows; skip them and you're paying the ECB/DNB tax in manual rework instead.

3. Teamwork is the multiplier — bright people, aligned around the same model, are the difference between a project that survives its own regulatory deadline and one that doesn't.

None of this was magic. It was structure — a model everyone could read, tools that respected it, and a team that trusted it enough to build on top of it instead of around it.
