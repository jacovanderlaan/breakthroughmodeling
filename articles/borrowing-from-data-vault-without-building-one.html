Borrowing from Data Vault Without Building One

2025-09-21 · 5 min read

How to apply the best ideas of Data Vault while staying grounded in a business-oriented, 3NF bi-temporal model

!Borrowing from Data Vault without building one — a Data Vault door with hub, link, and satellite pieces feeding into four business-oriented pillars/borrowing-from-data-vault-without-building-one/assets/01_hero/01_hero.png)

Summary

Data Vault has real ideas about historization, traceability, and scalability. But it's not always the right foundation if your core is a business-oriented LDM with 3NF and bi-temporality — the source-centric shape that makes Data Vault scale can also be the thing that makes it hard to query and expensive to run.

In this article I show how to selectively borrow the best Data Vault concepts — hubs, links, satellites — without adopting the full methodology. Structure beats magic here too: you don't need Data Vault's ceremony to get its discipline, as long as you keep the structure it was protecting.

What Data Vault Gets Right

Data Vault was designed around integration, historization, and auditability, and it earned its influence by solving real problems that older warehouse designs handled poorly. Four ideas hold up on their own merit:

  • Business keys in hubs — clear, stable anchor points for integration, independent of any one source system.
  • Links for relationships — associations between entities modeled explicitly, not buried in foreign keys that only make sense inside one source.
  • Satellites for history — attribute-level historization that can evolve independently of the entity it describes.
  • Auditability — full lineage back to source, which matters the moment compliance or a regulator asks "where did this number come from."

These aren't quirks of the methodology — they're good data modeling, and that's exactly why I keep reaching for them even outside a Data Vault project.

Where Literal Data Vault Breaks Down

The trouble isn't the ideas. It's what happens when you turn all four into a mandatory, wall-to-wall pattern applied everywhere, on every entity, regardless of fit. In practice I've seen the same three failure modes recur:

  • It drifts source-centric. Hubs proliferate around what the source systems happen to expose, not around what the business actually means by "customer" or "agreement."
  • The hub/link/satellite count compounds. What was three tables in a 3NF model becomes a dozen Data Vault objects, and every one of them needs its own load pattern, tests, and lineage.
  • Retrieval gets expensive without a business-shaped integration layer on top. Analysts querying raw Data Vault tables end up re-deriving the business view every time, because the vault itself was never meant to be queried directly.

None of this is a knock on Data Vault as a methodology — it's a warning about applying any methodology past the point where it's earning its cost. I worked through this tension in more depth in How Hans Hultgren and Ensemble Modeling Influenced My Data Architecture Philosophy — ensemble modeling shaped how I think about integration, without me adopting the entire Data Vault stack to get there.

Borrowing the Good, Skipping the Baggage

In practice, we don't "do Data Vault." We keep a business-oriented LDM as the backbone and borrow four Data Vault ideas into it, each mapped onto something that already exists in the model:

  • Hubs → the business entities already defined in the LDM (Customer, Product, Agreement). No separate hub layer — the entity is the anchor.
  • Links → relationships already explicit in the enterprise ontology (Customer–Agreement, Product–Event). If the relationship matters to the business, it's already modeled; Data Vault just confirms it deserves its own object.
  • Satellites → bi-temporal SCD2 historization on the entity itself, which I laid out in From Historization to Exposure: Building a Usable Data Platform with Dual SCD2. This is where dual SCD2 does the same job a satellite would, without a second physical layer to maintain.
  • Metadata-driven mapping → the automation backbone, in line with Metadata-Driven Data Architecture: Leveraging draw.io and VSCode for Scalable Design. This is the tool-extender part: draw.io and VSCode aren't Data Vault tools, but the metadata they capture drives the same kind of deterministic, repeatable mapping Data Vault automation promises.

The result reads like Data Vault's discipline applied to a business-shaped integration layer instead of a source-shaped one — you get the traceability without paying for a parallel object model.

DV in the Four Pillars

Selective borrowing isn't a one-off trick — it fits directly into the four-pillar shape this series has been building:

  • Business-Oriented LDM (Pillar 1) keeps hubs and links anchored in business meaning, not source topology.
  • Model-Driven Engineering (Pillar 2) automates the Data-Vault-inspired mappings so satellites and links don't become hand-maintained overhead.
  • Business-Friendly Mapping (Pillar 3) keeps lineage and historization legible to business users, not just to the engineers who built the pipeline.
  • UDMs (Pillar 4) give hubs and links a universal shape to connect back to, so the same pattern repeats across domains instead of being reinvented per project.

Treated this way, Data Vault ideas stop being a competing methodology and become tools inside a model-driven data architecture that was already business-first to begin with.

Key Takeaways

  • Data Vault's real contribution is design discipline: hubs, links, satellites, and auditability are good ideas independent of the methodology around them.
  • Literal, wall-to-wall Data Vault creates overhead and source-centricity once it's applied past the point of business fit.
  • Borrowing selectively keeps Data Vault's strengths while anchoring everything in a 3NF, bi-temporal, business-oriented model — dual SCD2 does the satellite's job without a second layer.
  • This isn't a workaround — it's how Data Vault ideas integrate into the four-pillar, model-driven architecture the rest of this series builds toward.
