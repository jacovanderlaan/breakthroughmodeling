Ensemble Modeling vs. Business-Oriented Data Modeling: Two Paths to Better Data Models

2025-09-19 · 5 min read

Hans Hultgren's Ensemble Modeling and Alec Sharp's Business-Oriented Data Modeling get filed as rival methods. They're not. One optimizes for what happens to the model after the workshop; the other optimizes for what happens inside it. Most teams need both.

Summary

Ensemble Modeling (ELM) and Business-Oriented Data Modeling (BODM) are two influential approaches to building logical data models — and they get compared more often than they get combined.

Ensemble Modeling emphasizes agility, decomposition, and alignment with implementation patterns like Data Vault or Anchor Modeling. Alec Sharp's Business-Oriented Data Modeling emphasizes clarity, communication, and business stakeholder engagement. Neither is wrong; they're solving different halves of the same problem. This article walks through both, where they diverge, and why sequencing them beats picking one.

What Is Ensemble Modeling?

Ensemble Modeling — the Ensemble Logical Model (ELM) — is Hans Hultgren's approach to logical modeling: business-driven and pattern-aware at the same time. It's built to survive contact with real Data Vault or Anchor Modeling implementations, not just to look good on a whiteboard.

The core mechanics:

  • Core Business Concepts (CBCs): the fundamental, stable entities — Customer, Product, Policy — that don't change even when the business around them does.
  • Natural Business Relationships (NBRs): the relationships between those concepts, captured directly from how the business actually talks about them, not from a normalization exercise.
  • Unified Decomposition™: breaking concepts into modular components — concepts, relationships, events — so each piece can evolve independently without cracking the model around it.
  • Business Workshops: collaborative sessions where definitions, terms, and relationships get captured with the people who own the meaning.
  • Implementation Readiness: ELM logical models map cleanly to physical ensemble patterns — Data Vault, Anchor Modeling, Focal Point — so historization, flexibility, and scale are designed in, not bolted on later.

The design target is change and longevity: a model built to absorb new requirements without losing semantic integrity every time the business shifts.

What Is Business-Oriented Data Modeling?

Business-Oriented Data Modeling (BODM), Alec Sharp's method, starts from a different question. It treats the model as a business communication tool first, a technical artifact second.

Its hallmarks:

  • Business-Friendly Diagrams: models a business stakeholder can actually read, without excessive normalization or technical shorthand getting in the way.
  • Shared Vocabulary: definitions and term clarity front and center, so the model reflects what the business means, not what a modeling tool defaults to.
  • Workshops & Facilitation: the same collaborative instinct as ELM, but aimed at aligning IT and business around one shared picture rather than at decomposing structure.
  • Focus on Usability: the model exists to clarify requirements, communicate scope, and support decisions — it isn't trying to prescribe a physical implementation.

In short: BODM optimizes for alignment, understanding, and communication — the conversation the model enables, not the schema it eventually becomes.

Comparing the Two Approaches

Both approaches take business engagement seriously — that's exactly why they get confused for competitors. But they're solving for different outputs. ELM is built to answer "how does this model survive the next five years of change and get implemented in Data Vault?" BODM is built to answer "does the business actually recognize itself in this model, right now?" One is a destination concern, the other is a conversation concern — and a model that nails one and skips the other tends to fail quietly, months later, in the direction it didn't cover.

When to Use Which?

  • Use Ensemble Modeling when you need long-term resilience in the data architecture and you already know you're implementing in Data Vault or Anchor Modeling patterns. It earns its keep in environments where requirements change often and the model has to keep up without a rebuild.
  • Use Business-Oriented Data Modeling when the priority is engagement, clarity, and stakeholder alignment — especially at the start of an initiative, or with a mixed audience that needs to see the big picture before anyone touches implementation.

Why Not Both?

These aren't mutually exclusive — they're sequential. Combining them gets you the best of both:

1. Start with Business-Oriented Data Modeling to build a clear, shared understanding of business concepts, terms, and scope — the conversation that gets everyone looking at the same thing.

2. Transition into Ensemble Modeling to decompose and structure those concepts into something that scales into a robust physical implementation.

Layer Sharp's communicative style on top of Hultgren's pattern-driven methodology and you get models that resonate with business users and hold up once they're actually built.

This is Structure Beats Magic applied to modeling itself: the magic isn't in picking the "right" methodology, it's in the structure of the sequence — conversation first, decomposition second, each step handing the next one something solid to work with.

Closing Thoughts

Ensemble Modeling and Business-Oriented Data Modeling look like they're aiming at different goals — technical adaptability versus business clarity. In practice, they're two halves of the same job. Skip the BODM half and you get a technically elegant model nobody outside the modeling team can validate. Skip the ELM half and you get a model everyone understands and nobody can safely evolve.

Data modeling was never just about diagrams and databases. It's about people, concepts, and the discipline to keep both usable as things change. Whether you're standing up the next data platform or running a business workshop, sequencing these two approaches — rather than choosing between them — is what gets you a model that's both understood and durable.
