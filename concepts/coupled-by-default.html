Key concept Anti-pattern

Coupled By Default

The anti-pattern: systems reach into each other's internals because nothing stopped them — dependencies nobody declared and nobody can see.

Nobody decided to couple these systems. Nothing prevented it. A report reads a source table directly because it was there. A pipeline joins another team's staging area because it was convenient that sprint. A dashboard depends on a column whose owner has never heard of the dashboard.

The dependency is real, load-bearing, and undeclared — which means it is invisible until it breaks, and it breaks at the worst possible moment: when the owner of the upstream, acting entirely reasonably, changes something they had every right to change.

The asymmetry that makes it spread

Coupling is cheap to create and expensive to remove. Reaching directly into a table takes an afternoon; the contract, the interface and the monitoring take a week. Under delivery pressure the afternoon always wins, and each individual shortcut is defensible.

And the cost lands on someone else, later. The person who couples gets the benefit; the person who must change the upstream pays. Nothing in the normal process makes that trade visible at the moment of the decision — so it recurs, forever, without anyone behaving badly.

How you know you have it

- Nobody can say who consumes a given table, so nothing can safely be deprecated. - Every change is preceded by an archaeology phase. - "We can't change that, something might depend on it" — an admission that dependencies are unknown, not that they are important. - A staging or intermediate layer has quietly become a public interface because people started reading it. - Fixing anything requires knowing everything, which is Bus Factor of One wearing an architecture diagram.

Not the same as "too many connections"

Decoupled systems can be densely connected. The distinction is not *how many* dependencies exist but whether they are declared, owned and monitored. Ten contracts are healthier than three undocumented reach-ins. The goal is front doors, not fewer visitors — see Decoupling.

The correction

You do not fix this by rewriting everything. You fix it by making the seams explicit where change actually happens: name the interface, agree the contract, monitor it, and let the internals behind it move freely. Start where it hurts — the boundaries that have already broken twice.

The permanent guard is default-deny: internals are private unless published. A system with no declared outbound interface offers nothing; if someone needs it, that is a conversation, and the conversation produces a contract.
