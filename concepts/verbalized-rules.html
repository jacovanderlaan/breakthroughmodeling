Key concept Semantics

Verbalized Rules

Business rules written as structured sentences that a business person can sign off and an agent can read, each carrying whether it is proposed, decided or derived. The sentence is the rule, not a comment on it.

> Business rules written as structured sentences that a business person can sign off and an agent can read, each carrying whether it is proposed, decided or derived. The sentence is the rule, not a comment on it.

Most rule layers are written for one reader. Rego, SQL or a rules engine serve the machine; a policy PDF serves people. Neither serves the other, so every rule exists twice and the two copies drift. Verbalized Rules write the rule once, as a structured sentence in the business's own vocabulary. SBVR and the verbalizations of fact-oriented modelling (FCO-IM) are the established forms: *a claim that is filed more than 30 days after the incident must be reviewed by a senior assessor.* The business can check it word by word, an LLM reads it without translation, and a generator can compile it into a check.

The second half is the rule's epistemic status. Ronald Ross makes the point with a triple: *the moon is made of cheese* is a valid RDF statement, and it is not a business fact. A verbalized rule says what kind of statement it is. It may be proposed (an AI extracted it from a document and nobody has accepted it yet), decided (the business ruled on it) or derived (another rule computes it). That status lets an agent tell a policy from a guess, and it keeps AI-harvested rules out of production until a person accepts them.

This matters now because agents read text. In the guardrails debate Ross argues that agent policies belong in natural-language business rules, not in policy code that only engineers can read. Verbalized Rules pair with Rules as Data: the sentence is the readable face, the rule record the executable one, and both come from the same source.
