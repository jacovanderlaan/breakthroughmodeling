Key concept Method

Model The Decision

Data models describe what exists; a decision model describes what you do with it — which question is asked, from which inputs, under which rules, with which outcome. Model the decision too (DMN), and AI gets a place inside it instead of in place of it.

> Data models describe what exists; a decision model describes what you do with it — which question is asked, from which inputs, under which rules, with which outcome. Model the decision too, and AI gets a place inside it instead of in place of it.

Most modeling work stops at the data: entities, attributes, relationships, a glossary. The decisions the business takes *on* that data — approve this claim, route this ticket, flag this invoice — usually live in someone's head, in a spreadsheet of exceptions, or buried in code. That is the Decision Black Box: the outcome is recorded, the logic is not.

The OMG standard DMN (Decision Model and Notation) gives the decision its own model, at two levels. A decision requirements diagram shows which decisions exist, which input data and which sub-decisions each one needs, and which knowledge sources (a policy, a regulation, an expert) define it. Underneath, the decision logic — most often a decision table: conditions in columns, one rule per row, a hit policy saying what happens when several rules match — states exactly how the outcome follows from the inputs. Business people can read it, and the same table can be executed.

This matters more now that AI makes decisions cheap. A typed AI model (an LLM with structured output, or a classifier like TypeSafe's Jev) is very good at the *fuzzy input* — "is this email a complaint?", "which category is this receipt?" — and unreliable at the *logic* — "complaint plus premium customer plus second time this month means call within a day". In a decision model the fuzzy questions become input decisions answered by the model, with a confidence threshold; the logic stays in the table, versioned like any rule as data; and low-confidence cases route to a person. The model reads; the table decides.

It also makes the modeling prerequisite visible. The options an AI may choose from are a controlled vocabulary that has to be designed with the business — an independent test of Jev found that swapping the option labels changed a third of its answers. The glossary, the decision table and the label set are one piece of work.

Beyond the decision table: rulings, approvals and intent

The decision table covers decisions that run every day. A second kind of decision is taken once and then governs the model: a ruling on how to treat a case, an approval of a mapping, the intent behind a boundary. These belong in the model too, versioned with the logic they govern. Peter Avenant observes that a silver layer rarely stalls on hard logic; it stalls on decisions nobody has taken, so *silver is a decision, not a transformation*. In another post he shows an approval that did not survive a source change, because it lived in a tool and not in versioned metadata. Andrew Smailes argues for scoping semantic layers by the business decisions they serve rather than by a glossary. Simon Späti names the cost for AI-built systems: when nobody knows the system, the intent behind it is gone. Every ruling, approval and decided boundary is a model element with a date, an owner and a reason, which is the positive form of the Decision Black Box and the data-level sibling of Architectural Decision Records.
