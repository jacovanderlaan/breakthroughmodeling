Key concept Architecture

Identity First

> Settle shared identity (business keys, identifiers, crosswalks) before meaning and rules. An ontology can define 'customer' perfectly and still fail when two systems cannot agree which customer a row is.

Tony Seale puts it bluntly: ontology has solved meaning; shared identity is the harder half. A definition of *customer* tells you what a customer is. It does not tell you that customer 4711 in the CRM and debtor 0815 in finance are the same party. Without that, every join across systems is a guess, and an agent makes that guess silently, usually by matching on names.

Data Vault practitioners know this order from the hub: the business key comes first, and everything else hangs off it. Identity First generalises the hub into a rule for the whole modelling sequence: identity, then meaning, then rules. SriniRao Seelam describes the same order for enterprise context layers. Identity includes the uncomfortable cases: Eby Kuriakose shows how hierarchies fork, with the same product or organisation rolling up differently in two systems. The answer is a governed crosswalk: a versioned, owned mapping that agents can read as context, not a lookup table someone maintains by hand.

Key design is part of it. A key is good when its uniqueness rule is stable, not when its value looks clean; the discussion of structured versus surrogate keys turns on that point. For agents the test is concrete: given two records from two systems, can the model say whether they are the same thing, and on what grounds?
